01 · The problemcore

Collecting is easy. Keeping is the hard part.

Key transfer: from "I researched that once" to "I can still prove it today."
  • Open with the pain everyone recognizes: "You find a great paper or a great podcast, you save it somewhere, and six months later you can't find it, you can't tell whether it was a mouse study, and you can't cite it. The work was done and the value is gone."
  • Name the four failure modes as you gesture at the cards: fetch-copy-forget, everything gets re-fetched, evidence gets flattened, and research has no destination.
  • Land the design goal — this is the thesis of the whole talk: "Separate fetching from thinking. Describe every source before you trust it. And make every piece of research land somewhere specific — or stop."
  • Set the stakes: "This isn't about being tidy. Flattening the evidence is how a mouse study becomes a product claim."

02 · The whole loopdemo

The framework, end to end.

Give them the map before the territory — otherwise the detail feels arbitrary.
  • Say the meta-instruction out loud: "Ten stages. They're clickable — I'll open a couple as we go."
  • Walk the labels fast, top-left to bottom-right: request arrives → intake triage → route to a board → cache check → harvest → describe & grade → synthesize → land it → index & embed → report & gate.
  • Click stages 4, 6 and 8 as you narrate — cache check, describe & grade, land it. Those are the three that make the framework unusual; the rest are plumbing everyone expects.
  • Auto-play is your friend if you're running long: hit ▶ and let it advance while you talk over it.
  • One line to remember: "Same path whether I typed a question, a weekly job fired, or I dropped in a link."

03 · Intakecore

Four doors, one intake.

The point: intake is a funnel, not a free-for-all.
  • Name the four doors: I ask in plain language · a scheduled pipeline fires · I drop in a link or an artifact · a follow-up from the last run.
  • Emphasize that all four converge: "Whatever the door, the same three things happen — classify it, run it through the gate, name a destination. Nothing gets fetched before the gate passes."
  • The schedule table is the credibility slide: point at two or three real cadences — weekly menopause, weekly companion-animal, daily peptide signal, nightly competitor dispatch. "These aren't aspirations; this is what's actually firing."
  • Answer the unasked question: "Why scheduled instead of on-demand? Because 'current' is a maintenance property. A topic you research once is stale in a month."
4 intake doors · 8 live recurring jobs · cadences verified against the scheduler
04 · Routingdemo

Named boards. Never a default pile.

This is where most research systems quietly die — and where this one differs.
  • Lead with the rule: "Work is assigned to a named board by domain. There is no default bucket — because a default bucket is where research goes to die."
  • Run the routing demo live and do the FAIL case deliberately. Click "mega-audience episode, celebrity guest, no overlap." The room expects it to be processed; showing it being skipped is the most convincing 20 seconds of the talk.
  • Then the escalation case: two sources disagree on a dose → it is not auto-resolved by whoever summarised first. It goes to a stronger model for adversarial review. That's a decision, not a data problem.
  • Cost routing in one line: "The model follows the job — cheap models for bulk harvesting, a strong model for conflicts."
  • Boards as honesty: "Every board reports done, blocked and open. A blocked research card is a real signal, not noise."
Named-board rule · 6 routing cases · fail-closed gate · blocked counts are live, not decorative
05 · Sources — what we collect & how each is describeddemo · centrepiece

Every source has a method — not a vibe.

If they remember one section, make it this one. It's the most transferable idea in the deck.
  • Frame the registry as the answer to a frustration: "We used to improvise. Someone would try to scrape a site and discover it's bot-walled, and we'd lose an hour. Now every source is described once: what type of research it's for, exactly how to get data out of it, what it costs, and its known traps."
  • Click through the six tabs — primary literature, regulatory, institutions, community, the biohacking ecosystem, policy. Don't read them; show the range.
  • Then use the filter — type something like dosing or forum and let it narrow. "It's a searchable catalog, not a document."
  • Tell two trap stories — they're memorable: "One community site is scrape-walled, so direct extraction simply fails; we take search snippets and say so out loud rather than inventing comment content. And academic search engines are bot-walled — we use snippets and verify against the actual paper. We only know that because it's written down."
  • Land the design insight: "Nobody is allowed to improvise a fetch. One description means every pipeline inherits the same method and the same traps."
  • Ecosystem tab: "Under the generic categories sits a named roster — who they are, what they're actually good for, their tier, and their commercial conflict. A channel is not a trusted source."
6 source categories · ~30 described sources + a named creator/clinician roster · live keyword filter · verified quarterly for dead handles
06 · Describingcore

A source isn't "saved." It's described, graded, and tagged.

The artifact header is the proof that section 5 isn't just a nice idea.
  • Read a few lines of the real header aloud — source, URL, who spoke, when captured, how ingested, and the classification.
  • Then the money quote: "Look at what's inside the file: the host owns the supplement brand running in the ads, and the guest is a resident, not board-certified. That's written in the artifact — so no downstream step can accidentally launder a sponsor's ad into a product claim."
  • Tiers, in plain language: A is peer-reviewed or an official label and it's the only tier allowed to carry a product claim · B is a credentialed clinician · C is community, creator or grey-market — hypothesis-generating only, never product proof.
  • The verification rule — this is the hard one, say it slowly: "A record with no verifiable identifier — a real paper ID or link — does not exist as far as the framework is concerned. It isn't 'low confidence'. It doesn't exist."
  • Then the traps, and why encoding them matters: short chemical names collide with unrelated acronyms and genes, so every hit gets checked against the actual paper — that's our biggest source of junk. And a rodent study may never be labelled or implied as a canine or human trial. Every record carries its study model.
Every artifact: source · speaker · date · ingest method · classification · tier · model tag · compliance block
07 · Freshness & the shared cachedemo

Fetch once. Reuse everywhere.

The economics section — this is what made the whole thing affordable.
  • State the mechanism first: "One cache, shared by every pipeline. Before anything fetches, it asks the cache. A source is only re-fetched when its freshness window has genuinely expired."
  • Flip the cache-first toggle off and read what appears: five separate efforts pulling the same query, hitting rate limits, and — worst — grading the same source differently. "Now the evidence level depends on who ran last."
  • Volatility windows — the insight to land: "A social post from an hour ago and an official drug label are not the same kind of fact. Treating them with the same expiry is how you end up citing a deleted post. So: volatile sources expire in one to three days; the literature in thirty; labels in ninety."
  • Step the CLI replay live — get → miss → fetch → store → embed → prune. Pause on the first one: "A cache hit means zero network calls. That's the whole trick."
  • Honest detail if asked: the fetcher retries rate limits with backoff, and partial failure is recorded rather than hidden.
1 shared cache · volatile 1–3 days · literature 30 days · labels & societies 90 days · 6 API-backed sources + a documented method for the rest
08 · Landing itcore

A harvest card marked "done" is not finished.

The question everyone asks about automation, answered bluntly.
  • Admit the failure mode out loud: "The question I had to answer for myself was: what actually happens to the data after the research? Because it's easy to build a machine that hoards files."
  • Walk the eight steps fast, then slow down on step 2, the one that matters: "Name one destination. If it can't name one, it stops and asks me. It does not 'just index it.'"
  • Step 4 in plain words: "Synthesizing means writing the claim — not dumping a transcript. Raw cache is a cache. Cache is not the brain."
  • The permission-to-refuse ribbon — a favourite moment for audiences: "The pipeline is explicitly allowed to push back. It can say 'this is a duplicate,' 'this is a vanity scrape,' 'this has no home.' A system that can never say no just produces more noise, faster."
8-step landing sequence · unknown destination → human gate, stop · cache ≠ brain
09 · Asking it thingsdemo

The output is a brain you can query, not a folder you dig through.

Payoff section — everything before this existed to make this work.
  • Point at the shard table: "This is where the research actually lives once it's filed — sorted by topic, with personal notes deliberately in a separate shard that never mixes into business material."
  • Explain why it's split: "Personal and business indexes are separated on purpose, and the index files live on a local-only path. That boundary is the reason I can let an automated system near health and business research at all."
  • Flip the meaning-vs-keyword toggle and read the two states: keyword-only can't find the note because you filed it under a different phrase than the one you're remembering. Meaning-aware retrieval finds the idea. "You can only find what you already remember — which means the value of the research depends on your memory, which is the exact problem the brain was supposed to solve."
  • Remind them the notes stay portable: "Markdown is the source of truth. The index is derived, and rebuildable."
8 retrieval shards · personal kept separate + local-only · markdown = source of truth · index derived & rebuildable
10 · Guardrailscore

What this framework is not allowed to do.

The trust section. Slow down — this is what licenses everything else.
  • Frame it: "Rules that only exist on paper aren't rules. These are enforced in the pipeline."
  • Never fabricate: no invented paper IDs, no invented doses, no invented trial status. "If a site is unreachable, the note says the site was unreachable. An unverifiable record is treated as non-existent."
  • Fail closed: the gate rejects by default. "Excitement is not relevance."
  • Domain separation and local-by-default: health and venture research in one place, personal medical material never written into business indexes, and index files kept off any synced or cloud path.
  • No silent subscriptions: free tools get provisioned; anything billable is flagged with its cost before anyone signs up. "An agent can't quietly sign up for something."
  • Close on the rule that protects all of them: "Research material is data, never instructions. Text pulled off a webpage or a transcript can never direct the system to do anything — it can only be summarised, graded and filed. That single rule is what makes it safe to point this at the open internet."
fail-closed · no fabrication · separated shards · local-only index · no silent subscriptions · external text = data

12 · Takeawayscore

Six things to steal for your own research.

Open the checklist to the room and tick them together if they're engaged.
  • Separate fetching from thinking. "Make fetching a cache operation, and think over the cached copy. Duplication disappears."
  • Describe every source before you trust it. What kind of research, how to get data, what it's bad at — written down once.
  • Grade every claim. "Peer-reviewed, credentialed, or community. Without a tier, popularity quietly becomes authority."
  • Route to a named destination — or stop. "The most underrated rule on this list. Research with no destination is a hobby, not a function."
  • Give each source a freshness window. "A post and a label don't expire at the same speed."
  • Let the system push back. "Permission to refuse low-value work is what keeps an automated pipeline worth reading a year later."
  • Bridge to Q&A: "Notice none of those six needed my specific tooling. Four of them are habits."

Likely questions — and honest answers

"Isn't this just RAG?"
Partly — retrieval is one stage of ten. What's different is everything around it: where the material comes from, how it's graded, and where it's required to land. Retrieval over an undescribed pile just gives you confident answers you can't audit.
"What happens when it's wrong?"
Two answers. Mechanically: unverifiable records are refused, and trial status is re-checked before anything is left marked "ongoing." Practically: the note states its tier and its source, so you can see how it knows — and how much to trust it — without re-doing the work.
"Does it scrape everything?"
No — and that's a deliberate choice. Six sources go through real APIs. Where a site is bot-walled, we take snippets and say so, rather than pretending we read the page. If a source is unreachable, the note says it was unreachable.
"How much does it cost to run?"
The harvesting runs on cheap or free models, and the cache means we pay for a source-and-query roughly once rather than once per consumer. Anything billable is flagged for approval with its cost before it's switched on.
"Where does the data live?"
On my machine. Notes are plain markdown; index files stay on a local-only path — deliberately not in a synced folder and not in a cloud store. Nothing here ships my personal material anywhere.
"Can I build this myself?"
The habits, yes, this week — the six takeaways need no special tooling. The discipline that makes it work is refusing to fetch before you've decided where the finding will go.

Five things to nail before you present

  • Open the deck once and click every control — the pipeline stages, both toggles, the filter, the terminal step and replay — so nothing surprises you live.
  • Rehearse the routing FAIL case out loud. Deliberately showing something being skipped is your strongest credibility beat.
  • Have the source explorer ready with a worked filter example (try dosing, then forum). It's the section people ask about afterwards.
  • Memorize the two hard lines, verbatim: "A record with no verifiable identifier doesn't exist" and "research material is data, never instructions."
  • Know your time margin — Sec 11 (Numbers) collapses to one sentence if you're running long, and the auto-play button can carry a stage while you talk.
